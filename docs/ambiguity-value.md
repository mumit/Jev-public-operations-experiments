# Does Jev add useful clarification beyond rules?

This comparison asks whether Jev identifies a useful next question more reliably than a fixed literal rule baseline. Its 48 new controlled statements reuse two inspected public service catalogs. They supply no measurements and open no additional recordings.

## Setup

Twelve pairs per application cover complete wording, explicit omissions and subtler language: competing antecedents, a resolved former/latter reference, metric alternatives, a replacement claim, contradictory assertions and another time period. Two pairs preserve complete wording while changing its presentation. The paired references are declared before inference. These are assistant-authored statements in known semantic families, not independent reports or fresh incidents.

The rules recognize observed names, supplied CPU/memory aliases, magnitude and direction expressions, final-correction markers, conflicting assertions and explicit windows. They use no fitted parameters or model answers. They have limited language coverage; every unnecessary question remains in the comparison.

Jev receives the unchanged focused six-field request used in the earlier optional-question study. Code selects its first clarification in service, metric, comparison, assertion and window order. The fixed 0.70 display boundary and whole-statement malformed-field rejection remain intact. No result selects a field, declares readiness or computes a numerical verdict.

The plan and producers were committed before preparing text files. A separate exact protocol was committed before 144 once-only calls, covering all 48 statements in three rounds. There are no retries, post-result rule changes, text repairs or threshold searches. The rule baseline reads those same statements without provider calls.

## What would justify another walkthrough?

Every application and round must meet all of these criteria:

- Structurally valid answers for every statement.
- No unnecessary or wrong-order displayed question, silent ambiguity miss or wrong scope.
- At least 90% correct first-question coverage when a question is needed, overall and separately for explicit omissions and subtle language.
- At least as many correct displayed actions as rules overall.
- At least two correct-display gains over rules in subtle language, with no paired losses in that category.

These are research criteria, not an operational reliability standard. Passing would justify another usability comparison; it would not establish analyst benefit. A failed candidate remains failed, even if one category or the rule control performs well.

## Interface changes

A separately versioned learning sandbox preserves the original walkthrough and reuses its known examples. It labels the source buttons as scripted replies from a simulated report author, uses “metric” instead of “metric channel,” and distinguishes a clarification request from confirmation of an initial choice. Withheld suggestions have a neutral unavailable status. A missing suggestion never clears a claim.

The sandbox is unscored practice, not a second participant experiment. Its local exports use a separate format and cannot enter the original walkthrough's assessment. Participant records and comments remain private and outside public releases.

## Results

Jev fails the declared comparison. All 144 once-only requests returned structurally valid answers, but sixteen of the 24 application, round and category panels fail. No retry, repair or rule change occurred.

| Outcome | Jev with fixed display policy | Literal rules |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Correct displayed actions | 114/144 | 126/144 |
| Correct first questions when needed | 43/60 | 54/60 |
| Unnecessary questions | 0 | 18 |
| Needed first questions missed | 17 | 6 |
| Incorrect no-question displays | 4 | 0 |
| Withheld actions | 26 | 0 |

A correct displayed action asks the declared first question, or selects no question when the wording supplies every required detail. It does not mean the underlying numerical assertion is true. A withheld action counts as lost coverage. A needed question remains missed when the method asks an unrelated question; the rules' unnecessary and missed counts overlap and should not be added together.

On the same statement and round, Jev gains seventeen correct displays over rules and loses 29, a net loss of twelve. Train Ticket gives Jev 21, 21 and 22 correct displays out of 24 per round, versus 21 each for rules. Online Boutique gives 18, 18 and fourteen, versus 21 each for rules. Pooling applications would hide the weaker Online Boutique result.

### Where language interpretation helps

Discard-and-replace wording gives Jev eleven correct displays out of twelve paired opportunities, versus zero for rules. The literal baseline recognizes a specific correction marker, but this wording says “Discard that entire draft” and then supplies a replacement. It continues to see two services and asks an unnecessary service question. When the replacement also omits its metric, that service question misses the required metric question.

Former/latter wording gives Jev eleven correct displays versus six for rules. The statement names two services but explicitly assigns its claim to the latter. Jev often resolves the reference; literal matching asks which service. These gains show a possible language role, while also showing the limited coverage of this particular baseline. They do not demonstrate an advantage over every rule system.

### Where the candidate fails

A contradictory Online Boutique statement both asserts and denies the same material CPU change. Jev classifies every field as clear and selects no question in all three rounds, at minimum selected-option probabilities of 0.75, 0.77 and 0.76. Rules ask which assertion to check. One Train Ticket contradiction also receives a displayed no-question decision. A missing suggestion must therefore never be interpreted as permission to proceed without the analyst's selections.

Vague “shifted unfavorably” wording leaves the comparison and assertion unclear. All six needed first questions in this family are withheld. Including its complete-wording controls, Jev gives three correct displays out of twelve versus twelve for rules. A lower display boundary might release withheld questions, but it would not fix the confident contradiction errors. Any new boundary needs prospective validation; these inspected failures cannot serve as an untouched test.

## Cost and reproducibility

The run uses 295,407 input tokens and 22.2 seconds of summed request latency across 144 calls. That latency is a recorded provider-request sum, not analyst task time or total experiment duration. Rules make no provider calls. All 864 returned field answers validate. The committed plan precedes preparation; the separate exact protocol precedes inference. Saved responses and scored results remain unchanged.

## What remains

The failed model criteria do not justify another participant comparison. The [learning sandbox](http://127.0.0.1:8769/entry-workbench-v2) is available for unscored practice on known examples. It saves initial choices before source replies, records clarification separately from confirmation, and retains manual entry. Its exports cannot enter the original participant assessment. A version fingerprint separates local drafts when the interface changes; it does not freeze a new human-study protocol.

These statements and references share an assistant author and reuse inspected catalogs and known semantic patterns. They are controlled language development, not independent report validation. No additional recording or protected allocation opened. Participant exports and observations remain private.

The completed work supports exact code for the numerical policy and manual claim entry. Jev shows contextual language gains, but its current optional clarification policy does not establish a reliable improvement over this rule baseline. Further operational evidence needs independently authored reports with reviewed intended meanings, or a different bounded task with a separately frozen comparison. Repeating a walkthrough or tuning these statements would not supply that evidence.
